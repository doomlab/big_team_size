\abstract{%
Specific disciplines have reported recent increases in scientific collaboration (i.e., co-authorship), which may be related to article metric benefits (i.e., increased citation rates). However, it is currently unclear what disciplines have seen increases in collaboration, and if this increase in collaboration is associated with increased citation rates. Additionally, little is known about how demographic characteristics of the authorship team (e.g., \emph{h}-index, gender, number of participating institutions) may influence article metrics in tandem with team size. Understanding the impact of collaboration patterns across time and on article metrics is critical given the importance of these factors in hiring and tenure decisions, and the general growth of the scientific enterprise. We used metadata provided by Elsevier's International Center for the Study of Research (ICSR) lab to investigate collaboration patterns across disciplines over time and to understand factors that influence article metrics. \maketitle

Scientific collaboration (i.e., co-authorship) has been increasing in many fields of study such as biomedical sciences\textsuperscript{1}, medicine\textsuperscript{2}, and psychology\textsuperscript{3}. Notably, scientific collaboration is one factor that may positively influence article metrics (e.g., citation rates\textsuperscript{4,5}), although the extent of this effect may vary by field (e.g., physics\textsuperscript{6}). Within scientific collaboration, additional characteristics of the authorship team may exert influence on these metrics. Authorship team diversity is a key factor one may consider when looking at the impact of collaboration on article metrics. Author diversity, broadly speaking, has been associated with positive outcomes such as publication in high impact factor journals, rates of citations, and funding\textsuperscript{7,8}. It is important to note that author diversity can be conceptualized along a continuum of factors such as: gender, physical location (e.g.~United States, Brazil), institutional affiliation, discipline, and rank (e.g., associate faculty). Evidence suggests an impact of international collaboration, rank, and gender on article metrics in fields such as sport science research\textsuperscript{9}, physics\textsuperscript{6}, and psychology\textsuperscript{10}, respectively, but a gap exists in which the accumulation of these factors over time and across disciplines has not yet been addressed. As such, this project aims to understand: 1a.) What is the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time, 1b.) is team size related to article impact?, and 2.) What characteristics of team composition have the greatest influence on article metrics?

\emph{Question 1}. What is the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time, and is team size related to article impact?

Science is becoming increasingly collaborative; evidence for this trend includes an increase in co-authored peer-reviewed publications (compared to single-authored)\textsuperscript{5} as well as initiatives, calls, and funding opportunities for collaborative research from both private and public/government sectors. Further, some argue that scientific collaboration and large interdisciplinary/international teams are needed to effectively address modern societal questions and problems\textsuperscript{11}. Indeed, team size (i.e., number of co-authors) has been increasing in recent years\textsuperscript{1} across various disciplines including science and engineering, social sciences, and arts and humanities\textsuperscript{5}. While some research suggests an overall trend towards collaboration\textsuperscript{5} these findings do not appear to apply to all disciplines equally.

Within the natural/physical sciences, subdisciplines vary in their collaborative growth. For instance, average team size in physics has reportedly doubled over the last 45 years, whereas for mathematics, the growth appears slower - with the average team size rising from 1.22 to 1.845. Grossman\textsuperscript{12} corroborates this effect, noting that for mathematics the average number of collaborators has grown since the 1940s with 1 in 8 papers having 2+ authors. On the other hand, a review of four major musculoskeletal science journals\textsuperscript{13} noted approximately a doubling in the number of co-authors on a publication (3.67--7.3) over the last 30 years. Likewise, biology has noted decreases in single-authored papers across specific subareas (i.e., Ecology, Genetics, Zoology and Botany)\textsuperscript{14}.

Among the social sciences, a pattern of increasingly collaborative authorship emerges. For example, sociology has reported increases in both collaboration and the average number of authors per publication during the period from 1935 to 2005\textsuperscript{15}. Specific publication outlets in psychology (e.g., Journal of Applied Psychology)\textsuperscript{16} have seen increases in co-authored and decreases in solo-authored papers. Specific subfields of psychology have also noted similar changes in authorship trends (e.g., industrial-organizational psychology)\textsuperscript{17}. Various branches of business, such as economics\textsuperscript{18} and finance\textsuperscript{19} have also noted increases in co-authorship.

Practically speaking, collaboration might offer important benefits to authors, as number of authors appears to be associated with article impact (i.e., citation rates). Among publications indexed in the annual volumes of the Web of Knowledge (Thomson-Reuters) in 2007, single author papers received an average of 2.91 citations while multi-author papers exhibited an average of 5.39\textsuperscript{20}; this difference was consistent when examining citation averages from 1998 to 2007. Further probing this relationship it appears that the number of citations increases with the number of authors, up to a point (approx. 20 authors) after which the relationship becomes inconsistent. As such, collaboration may be rewarded by increased impact.

While most of the available research points to a positive relationship between collaboration and citations, the available research tends to examine manuscripts published in a narrow time frame (i.e., ten years)\textsuperscript{21}, and often looks at fields in isolation, specific journals\textsuperscript{17}, and/or specific geographical regions\textsuperscript{22}. As such, it is unclear how various disciplines have embraced collaboration over time and whether there has been any associated impact.

\emph{Question 2}. What characteristics of team composition have the greatest influence on article metrics?

Although several disciplines report increases in co-authorship over the past several decades, it is important to ask the question about who is publishing, and with whom. Generally speaking, despite increases in women's representation in academia, women still are underrepresented when it comes to publishing co- and solo-authored papers\textsuperscript{23}. In addition to authoring a smaller proportion of published work, research suggests women are also less likely to hold prominent authorship positions\textsuperscript{24,25}. For example, women are underrepresented in regard to publishing articles in specific areas of medicine\textsuperscript{26,27}, STEM fields (e.g., mathematics\textsuperscript{28}), and social sciences\textsuperscript{28,29}, although the extent of these differences varies by subfield (e.g., computational biology, as compared to biology in general)\textsuperscript{29}. Furthermore, women are less likely to receive credit for their contributions to empirical/scholarly written work\textsuperscript{30}. Interestingly, women are less likely to collaborate with previous co-authors compared to men\textsuperscript{31}; although it is not clear why this is the case, it could have important implications for their careers as novel teams tend to produce higher-impact work\textsuperscript{32}.

Compounding this lack of representation is a body of research suggesting that women's contributions to science via peer-reviewed publications are often perceived to have lesser value than publications authored by men. After controlling for year of publication, publication outlet, focus, theoretical framework, methodology, tenure status, and institutional affiliation, women were still cited less than men\textsuperscript{33}. Relevant to this manuscript is the body of research examining the association between gender and citation rates. Previous work has noted that in specific fields, men are less likely to cite women's work and work authored by women typically receives fewer citations overall\textsuperscript{34,35}. This difference in likelihood of accumulating citations could influence a researchers' impact\textsuperscript{36}, which is often considered important for career advances.

One objective measure of a scholar's output and impact is the ``\emph{h}-index''\textsuperscript{37}. The \emph{h}-index combines the number of articles published with the number of citations received for those articles. This approach attempts to balance high-impact articles with the number of articles published. Specifically, the \emph{h}-score is the number of articles (X) that receive X citations. The \emph{h}-index is one of the most widely used and accepted measures of a scholar's impact\textsuperscript{38}. Fields such as psychology have observed gender differences pertaining to \emph{h}-indices which favor men. Women professors have a significantly lower \emph{h}-index than men, with a difference of approximately four points. After controlling for the year of first publication, gender shares approximately 2.6\% of the variance with \emph{h}-index\textsuperscript{10}. This effect was examined relative to mean salaries for full professors, and practically, translated to an astounding effect of a \$13,100 a year difference between men and women\textsuperscript{10}.

It is important to note that there is evidence of increasing equity between genders in certain fields; in international relations, for example, the gap between men and women's manuscript submissions and publications are narrowing and there appears to be no significant relationship between gender and citation count\textsuperscript{39}. Notable, is that the calculation of \emph{h}-indices may be particularly biased by self-citations and years without publication. Higher rates of citations received by men were partially explained by high rates of self-citation which altered scores up to 14 extra \emph{h}-index points. Women also engaged in self-citation, although less so, and only impacting their \emph{h}-index by up to 5 points\textsuperscript{34}. Although there are alternatives to the \emph{h}-index that account for self-citations (e.g.~the \emph{b}-index\textsuperscript{40}), the \emph{h}-index is popular and generally accepted as a robust bibliometric tool.

Given the arguments regarding the necessity of scientific collaboration and large international teams to address modern societal questions and problems\textsuperscript{11}, it may be intuitive that institutional and geographical diversity is on the rise. One source notes an increase in articles that have the presence of both a U.S. author and at least one international co-author for psychology\textsuperscript{41}. Similar increases in the fraction of internationally co-authored papers (i.e., ratio of co-authored articles with international institutions to the total number of co-authored articles with domestic and international institutions) were noted in other scientific disciplines such as mathematics, biology, medicine, and engineering\textsuperscript{42}. However, while it appears most fields are increasing international engagement, applied fields (especially medical sciences) have higher rates of growth\textsuperscript{43}.

Collaboration involving those outside of one's home institution and country may significantly impact article metrics, which may be one reason for the increasing rates of international collaboration. For instance, for UK institutions indexed in the Science Citation Index between 1981 and 1994, collaboration within one's home university or country resulted in a small increase in average impact (0.75 citations) whereas collaboration outside of one's home country resulted in a more moderate increase (1.6 citations)\textsuperscript{44}. Indeed, both well-established and young institutions benefit from international collaboration as evidenced by a citation per paper increase when the authorship team is international\textsuperscript{45}. This benefit is corroborated by the observation that international co-authorship publications are over-represented in the ESI global top 10 \% highly cited publications relative to all journal publications from the same institution\textsuperscript{45}. Indeed, article impact in terms of citations per paper seem to be greatest for those manuscripts with multiple domestic and international collaborators, and smallest for those with no institutional collaboration.

However, this trend may not be consistent across fields, journals, and/or countries, and warrants further investigation (e.g., physics)\textsuperscript{6,46}. For example, international collaboration generally increases citation rates in the field of sports science but the extent of this effect varies by which countries are collaborating\textsuperscript{9}. Collaboration between the United States and Canada for instance, produced a mean citation rate greater than the domestic mean observed citation rate of each country respectively, whereas collaboration between the United Kingdom and Greece produced a mean citation rate that was lower than the domestic mean observed citation rate of each country respectively. Furthermore, when examining the benefits of international collaboration on citation count for american authors publishing in the top three interdisciplinary journals (\emph{Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences} {[}PNAS{]}, \emph{Nature}, and \emph{Science}), there is an increased citation rate for international collaboration when compared to American-only authored papers\textsuperscript{46}. It is important to note that this effect was the result of a significant increase in citation rate for international papers published in \emph{PNAS} only. When examining each journal independently, no benefit of international collaboration was found for \emph{Science} or \emph{Nature}. Given the accelerated growth of international collaboration across various fields over time\textsuperscript{43,47}, it is increasingly important to understand the influence of this diversity on article metrics.

The majority of work examining team characteristics and article metrics have focused on a specific field\textsuperscript{6}, and/or a limited/restricted number of team characteristics (e.g., size)\textsuperscript{5}. Empirical data comparing the impact of diverse/multiple team characteristics on article metrics within a given field is limited\textsuperscript{2,6,9,12,14}. Along a similar vein, empirical data comparing the impact of team characteristics on article metrics across fields is also limited (for notable exceptions, see Larivière et al\textsuperscript{4} or Wuchty et al\textsuperscript{5}). Comparing outcomes within and across fields can allow for an increased understanding of these fields' scientific culture. Practically, understanding factors associated with an article's impact can be important for researchers' outreach, as well as planning for the job market and tenure review.

\section{Method}\label{method}}

\hypertarget{ethics-information}{%
\subsection{Ethics Information}\label{ethics-information}}

All of the data for this manuscript is drawn from peer reviewed journals. All of the extracted meta-data from the individual publications is de-identified in the database. As such, per US Federal Guidelines, this research is not subject to human ethics review boards (§46.101, (b) (4)).

\hypertarget{design}{%
\subsection{Design}\label{design}}

This study was an archival data investigation into scholarly publication records using Elsevier's curated database Scopus. This study was conducted through access to Elsevier's International Center for the Study of Research Lab (ICSR; \url{https://www.elsevier.com/icsr}). The ICSR is designed to support researchers' in their empirical study of meta-science on large datasets using the Databricks platform running Apache Spark architecture for big-data analyses. Through ICSR, researchers can obtain access to full publication metadata from Scopus including article metrics, author(s), and affiliation information. Scopus is a global database on abstracts and citations, containing a wide representation of content including articles, books, and conference proceedings\textsuperscript{48}. Scopus contains over 80 million entries from 1788 to present curated by a team of experts to ensure quality metadata and inclusion of appropriate publications. All variables extracted from this dataset are described below.

\hypertarget{variables}{%
\subsubsection{Variables}\label{variables}}

Article characteristics included the year of publication, number of authors (team size), and discipline. Discipline will be defined at the All Science Journal Classification (ASJC; \url{https://service.elsevier.com/app/answers/detail/a_id/15181/supporthub/scopus/}) using the five global subject area codes (Health, Physical, Life, Social Sciences, and Multidisciplinary){[}\^{}We originally planned on using the 27 subfields within the ASJC. However, we updated our preregistration to use only five of the larger subfields due to computational constraints{]}. Metric characteristics included citation count (number of times an article was cited within the current data snapshot) and weighted citations. Field weighted citations indicates the number of citations in the last five years. We excluded articles within the last five years for the field weighted citation counts to ensure consistency for this variable.

Author characteristics included affiliation country, affiliation/institution, \emph{h}-index, gender, and gender ratio. We transformed affiliation country into a count variable of the number of unique countries. Similarly, we transformed affiliation/institutions into a count of the number of different affiliations across authors. We used the \emph{h}-index of the first and last authors for each article, with and without self-citation. Last, Elsevier has noted that their inferred gender column has a 97\% accuracy, and we used the inferred gender of the first and last authors for each article. From this variable, we additionally calculated the gender ratio (number of inferred male / total authors) as a single variable for each article.

\hypertarget{sampling-plan}{%
\subsubsection{Sampling Plan}\label{sampling-plan}}

While Scopus includes articles from 1788 to present, we limited our sample to journal articles published from 1970 until present. Scopus indicates that articles from 1970 to present include reference information, which will be required for calculation of several of the variables described above. Only journal articles from this time period will be included sampled at a rate of 25\% of eligible articles. We will include the date of analysis to indicate what data was available at the time analysis{[}\^{}We originally included all articles from 1970 and forward. Due to computational constraints, we randomly sampled 25\% of eligible articles during this time span to constrain the run time of our code and memory necessary to calculate \emph{h}-index. It is important to note that we did \emph{not} calculate \emph{h}-index on the randomly sampled dataset. These values were calculated on the full data and then merged with the randomly sampled data to ensure \emph{h}-indices were appropriate for authors on the randomly sampled data{]}.

\hypertarget{power}{%
\subsubsection{Power}\label{power}}

Given the large size of the Scopus dataset, all analyses are expected to achieve the required 95\% power. Analyses using the \emph{pwr} library\textsuperscript{49} indicate that planned regressions have the ability to detect effects at \(R^2\) \textless{} .00001 at 95\% power using a 95\% confidence interval.

\hypertarget{data-screening}{%
\subsubsection{Data Screening}\label{data-screening}}

Missing data was excluded for the analyses in which the data was missing only. We assumed all data is accurate. In all analyses, we examined the cell counts for each categorical variable in order to collapse small cell sizes (i.e., \emph{n} \textless{} 5). No articles will be considered an outlier, as all publication data was expected to be part of the population.

In each relevant analysis, we examined for multicollinearity of the IVs, as team size may positively correlate with count variables calculated in later research questions. If values are multicollinear, \emph{r} \textgreater{} .90, we planned to note these correlations and only use one of the proposed variables. No values reached this criteria for the five subject areas. The size of the dataset implied the assumption of normality. In our analyses, a linear trend was the main focus of interest, and we did not note any non-linear trends. Last, nearly all scatter plots indicated heteroscedasticity. Please see Appendix A for further details.

\hypertarget{decision-criteria}{%
\subsubsection{Decision Criteria}\label{decision-criteria}}

The large sample size was expected to indicate all predictors significant at the traditional \emph{p} value criterion. For individual predictors, we used the 95\% confidence intervals of the slope to indicate if the effect is non-zero (i.e., confidence interval does not include zero rounded to three decimals). The difference between effects will be determined by examining the confidence intervals of \(R^2\) to three decimal places calculated with the \emph{MBESS} library\textsuperscript{51}. If they do not overlap, we will state that they are different.

\hypertarget{research-questions}{%
\subsubsection{Research Questions}\label{research-questions}}

All analyses will be performed using \emph{pyspark}\textsuperscript{52} within the Databricks system. Visualization and presentation of analyses were included using \emph{R}\textsuperscript{53}, \emph{dplyr}\textsuperscript{54}, \emph{rio}\textsuperscript{55}, \emph{tidyr}\textsuperscript{56}, \emph{patchwork}\textsuperscript{57}, and \emph{ggplot2}\textsuperscript{58}.

\emph{Research Question 1a: What is the pattern of team size distribution within various disciplines over time?}

We first calculated the mean, median, and interquartile range of team size by subject area. We did not explicitly compare discipline intercepts in a \emph{posthoc} format with any form of test. Instead, we present visualizations to display the distribution and their interquartile range for readers to examine.

In order to determine if team sizes change overall across time, we used a linear model of team size predicted by year of publication in which each article was only represented once, regardless of discipline. We examined if the confidence interval of the slope parameter was different from zero to determine if the predictor was significant. Last, we calculated each subject area's slope and confidence interval using a linear model of team size predicted by year of publication. Again, we provide these slopes in a visualization for readers to examine with no official \emph{post hoc} comparison. While a multilevel model would allow us to embed all disciplines within one model, no \emph{pyspark} multilevel model implementation exists to model data at this scale.

\emph{Research Question 1b: Is team size related to article impact?}

For this research question, we examined the impact of team size on article publication metrics for impact. Our hypothesis was that team size impacts article metrics (non-directional). We ran these analyses once for each dependent variable of citation count and weighted citation five year count. We calculated the mean, median, and interquartile range of each publication metric by discipline. We provide visualizations of these values instead of comparing them directly.

To determine the impact of team size on publication metrics, we used a linear model of publication metrics predicted by time of publication (covariate) and team size. We included time as a covariate, as we expected that older articles will potentially have more or different metrics because they have been around longer to cite. We examined if the confidence interval of the team size slope parameter was different from zero. Last, we calculated the slope for team size for each subject area separately. We visualized these differences and the confidence intervals.

\emph{Research Question 2: What characteristics of team composition have the greatest influence on article metrics?}

For this research question, we continued to use the model from the previous research question and add other article metrics to determine their contribution to the model. Our hypothesis was that each variable has an impact on the metrics variables (non-directional). Each author independent variable will be modeled separately and compared using confidence intervals as described above to determine which variable had the strongest impact on publication metrics.

Using the linear model from the previous research question, we added each author independent variable (affiliation country count, affiliation/institution count, \emph{h}-index, gender (male versus female), and gender ratio) in separate models to calculate their slope. We determined if these slopes were important by examining if their confidence interval was greater than zero. We examined the effects of each author metric separately. When examining first and/or last author information, the single author paper presents a unique problem because they are both first and last author. Therefore, these cannot be included in the same model. In order not to use the same variance twice, single author papers will be treated as first author only, and missing data for last author analyses. If these were included in the same model, all single author papers would be dropped, which would bias the results. We will then compare each overall \(R^2\) to determine if their confidence intervals overlap. This analysis was used to indicate which author variable provided the most predictive information (since all other covariates are the same for each analysis, only the author variable is different). Last, we calculated each author independent variable separated by subject area and then visualized those \(R^2\) and confidence intervals for each variable.

The life sciences and multidisciplinary subject areas were both positive effects.

\textbf{Country and Institution}. Country and institution counts both increased the number of citations. These effects were always larger for the overall citations than the five year citation counts; however, the effect sizes were again always larger for five year citation models than overall citation models.

\textbf{h-index}. The general pattern for \emph{h}-index effects were positive with larger effects for no-self citations versus including self-citations and first author versus last author. Multidisciplinary papers and the social sciences also tended to have the largest increase in citation counts when authors with higher \emph{h}-indices were included on a manuscript. These coefficients were generally stronger for overall citations, but again, the effect sizes were often larger for five year citation patterns.

\subsubsection{Data Screening}\label{data-screening-1}}

You can find these results by going to \texttt{01\_prereg} and opening \texttt{team\_science\_analysis.html}. We ran these analyses on our first pilot code. They are more detailed than the second analysis code (i.e., we could pinpoint specific subareas within the final five areas analyzed), so we used the smaller analyses for data screening. The results are summarized here.

\emph{Multicollinearity}.

By searching for \texttt{stat.corr} within the code, you can find the correlations with there are multiple independent variables within a regression analysis. They are usually printed at the bottom of a scatter plot. The correlations for research question 1 were all very low \emph{r}s from .02 to .30. The correlations for research question 2 were similarly low with two exceptions: \emph{r} \textasciitilde{} .50-.53 for team size and number of countries and \emph{r} \textasciitilde{} .71-74 for team size and institution count. Given the number of countries and institutions can only be as high as the number of authors, these values are not surprising. Further, they did not meet our exclusion criteria of \emph{r} \textgreater{} .90 overall. Institution count for Earth Sciences correlated with team size at \emph{r} \textasciitilde{} .902-.908 but no other values were found. Given the more generalized subject areas, the average \emph{r} for these two variables was considered ok for use for the category that includes Earth Sciences.

\emph{Linearity and Homoscedasticity}.

By plotting the standardized residuals versus the predicted values, we examined for linearity (looking for curved patterns of dots) and homoscedasticity (looking for unequal spreads of dots on Y for each value of X). Each of these is shown in the scatter plots in the attached preregistration output noted above. You can view these plots, but an example is provided below. No graphs indicated curved relationships, but all graphs indicated some form of heteroscedasticity.
